# Vascular Anastomosis
In head and neck microvascular reconstruction using the radial forearm free flap, the selection of recipient vessels follows well-established patterns based on reliability, accessibility, and flow characteristics. In this setting, the facial artery is the most commonly used recipient artery because of its reliable caliber, good flow, and easy cervical access, typically allowing an end-to-end anastomosis. The superior thyroid artery is a frequent alternative, particularly when the facial artery is unavailable or previously ligated. Less commonly, the lingual artery may be used due to its proximity to the tongue defect, although its deeper location increases technical difficulty. In more complex situations, the external carotid artery (branch or trunk) may serve as a recipient, often requiring an end-to-side anastomosis, while the transverse cervical artery is rarely used and mainly reserved for vessel-depleted necks.
For venous outflow, branches of the internal jugular vein (IJV) represent the most common and preferred recipients, usually via the facial vein, common facial vein, or a direct IJV branch, owing to their high flow and low thrombosis risk. The facial vein is also frequently used, followed by the external jugular vein, which serves as a common secondary option, especially for a second vena comitans or the cephalic vein. The anterior jugular and retromandibular veins are used less often due to variable or anatomy-dependent reliability. In routine practice, the most common configuration is radial artery to facial artery with venous drainage to the facial vein or an IJV branch. Because the radial forearm free flap typically has two venae comitantes, many surgeons anastomose one vein to the IJV system and optionally a second to the EJV for added safety, with final vessel choice influenced by prior neck dissection, radiation history, and overall vessel quality and reach.
